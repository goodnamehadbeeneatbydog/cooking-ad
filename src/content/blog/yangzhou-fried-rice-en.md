If I had to choose one dish to represent the art of Chinese cooking, it might be Yangzhou Fried Rice. It sounds simple — rice, egg, some protein, a few vegetables — but getting it right requires technique. The rice should be fluffy and separate, each grain coated in egg and oil but not greasy. There's a reason this is the fried rice by which all others are judged.

I spent months trying to recreate the fried rice I had at a little restaurant in Yangzhou itself. The chef wouldn't give me his recipe, but he did watch me attempt it and pointed out three things I was doing wrong. I'll share those with you here.

## What Makes It Yangzhou Style

True Yangzhou Fried Rice has a specific ingredient list: shrimp, ham, egg, peas, and scallions. That's it. No carrots, no corn, no soy sauce flooding the rice. The flavor comes from the ingredients themselves and the technique, not from drowning everything in sauce.

The dish originated in Yangzhou, a city in Jiangsu province famous for its refined cuisine. It's said that a Qing dynasty chef created it to impress an emperor, which is a lot of pressure for a bowl of rice.

## The Rice Is Everything

Here's the first thing the chef corrected me on: **you must use day-old rice**. Freshly cooked rice has too much moisture and will clump together. Day-old rice has dried out slightly, which lets each grain fry separately.

If you don't have day-old rice, here's a workaround: cook your rice with slightly less water than usual, spread it on a baking sheet, and refrigerate it for at least 2 hours. It won't be quite as good, but it'll work.

Jasmine rice is traditional, but any medium-grain rice works. Avoid sushi rice (too sticky) and basmati (too fragrant and the grains are too long).

## Ingredients

- 4 cups cooked day-old jasmine rice, chilled
- 3 eggs, lightly beaten
- 1/2 cup small shrimp, peeled and deveined
- 1/2 cup Chinese ham or regular ham, diced small
- 1/2 cup frozen peas, thawed
- 3 scallions, finely chopped
- 3 tablespoons vegetable oil
- 1/2 teaspoon salt
- 1/4 teaspoon white pepper
- 1 teaspoon sesame oil

## The Three Secrets

**Secret #1: Hot wok, cold oil**

Your wok needs to be screaming hot before you add oil. I'm talking about wisps of smoke. Then add the oil and swirl it immediately. This creates a non-stick surface and gives you that wok hei — the smoky, slightly charred flavor that defines great Chinese cooking.

**Secret #2: Egg first, rice second**

Pour the beaten egg into the hot oil and let it set for about 5 seconds. Then add the rice on top of the partially set egg. As you stir-fry, the egg breaks into small pieces and coats each grain of rice. This is the "golden fried rice" technique, and it's what gives Yangzhou fried rice its signature color.

**Secret #3: Don't overcrowd**

If your pan is too full, the rice steams instead of fries. Cook in batches if you need to. Each grain should be toasted and separate, not soft and clumpy.

## Step-by-Step

**Step 1: Prep all ingredients**

Have everything measured and ready. Fried rice happens fast.

Break up any clumps in your cold rice with your fingers. Yes, it's messy, but it works better than a spoon.

**Step 2: Cook the shrimp**

Heat 1 tablespoon of oil in your wok over high heat. Add the shrimp and stir-fry for about 1 minute until just pink. Remove and set aside.

**Step 3: Scramble the egg**

Add another tablespoon of oil. Pour in the beaten egg. Let it set for 5 seconds, then immediately add the rice on top.

**Step 4: The main fry**

Start stir-frying, breaking up the egg and mixing it with the rice. Keep the heat high and keep things moving. You're looking for the rice to start "dancing" in the pan — jumping slightly as it hits the hot surface. This takes about 3-4 minutes.

**Step 5: Add the good stuff**

Add the ham, peas, and cooked shrimp. Keep stir-frying for another 2 minutes. The peas should be heated through, and the ham should start to crisp slightly at the edges.

**Step 6: Season and finish**

Add the salt and white pepper. Toss to distribute evenly. Turn off the heat, drizzle with sesame oil, and toss in the scallions. Give it one final mix and serve immediately.

## Common Mistakes

**Soggy rice**: Almost always caused by too much moisture. Use day-old rice, and don't add wet ingredients without draining them first.

**Bland flavor**: Salt early and salt enough. Fried rice needs more seasoning than you think because the rice itself is bland. The white pepper is crucial too — it adds a subtle heat that black pepper can't replicate.

**Greasy rice**: Too much oil. You need just enough to coat the pan. The rice shouldn't look shiny when you're done.

**Clumpy rice**: Either the rice was too fresh, or you didn't break up the clumps before adding it to the pan.

## Variations

Once you've mastered the classic, try these variations:

- **Chicken fried rice**: Substitute diced chicken breast for the shrimp
- **Vegetable fried rice**: Use diced carrots, corn, and green beans instead of meat
- **Beef fried rice**: Use thinly sliced flank steak, marinated in soy and cornstarch
- **Salted fish fried rice**: A Cantonese classic with salted fish and diced chicken

## The Soy Sauce Question

Traditional Yangzhou Fried Rice doesn't use soy sauce. The color comes from the egg, and the flavor comes from the ham, shrimp, and sesame oil. Adding soy sauce makes it taste like every other fried rice.

That said, if you really want to add a splash, use light soy sauce and add it to the side of the wok so it sizzles and caramelizes slightly. Dark soy sauce will make your rice look muddy.

## Storage and Reheating

Fried rice keeps well in the fridge for 3 days. It's actually one of the best meal-prep options because the flavors continue to develop.

For reheating, a hot pan with a tiny bit of oil works best. The microwave makes it soft and sad. If you must use a microwave, put a damp paper towel over the bowl to prevent drying out.

## Final Thoughts

Yangzhou Fried Rice taught me that the simplest dishes often require the most skill. There's nowhere to hide — every ingredient and every technique is on full display. But that's also what makes it so satisfying to master.

Start with day-old rice, get your wok hot, and take your time. The perfect fried rice is closer than you think.
